# GTM Laws, Adapted

Source: "The 48 Laws of SaaS", a generic B2B SaaS list. It was written for a
company selling seats to a business buyer, with a sales team, a procurement
cycle, and no regulator anywhere near it. UpSpeech has three parties in the loop
and a deliberately conservative claims posture, so importing the list unchanged
would drag in advice that breaks `brand-voice.md` and the Class I position.

All 48 are kept here and sorted:

- **Adopt** (23). True as written. The line underneath is the UpSpeech version.
- **Rewrite** (16). Right instinct, wrong shape for a clinical product. Our
  version replaces it.
- **Reject** (5). Do not import. The reason is written down so nobody
  re-imports them in six months.
- **Not yet** (4). Reasonable advice with no machinery to act on it. Revisit
  when there is pricing, paid spend, or a sales function.

Read `brand-voice.md` before acting on any of this. Where a law and the
sensitivity rules disagree, the sensitivity rules win.

## The three parties

Every law below has to be read against who is who.

| Role     | Who                                           | What they feel                                                                                |
| -------- | --------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Buyer    | The clinic, via the owner or practice manager | Capacity, differentiation, staff time                                                         |
| Champion | The speech-language pathologist               | The evening lost to session notes, walking into an appointment with no idea how the week went |
| User     | The patient                                   | Practising alone, with nothing coming back                                                    |

The pilot settled that the buyer and the user are different people, and that
willingness to pay sits with the clinic. Patient willingness to pay is real
(roughly 3 to 15 euros a month, with one unprompted floor at 3 to 4) but the
therapists' own framing was that the clinic should be the one buying.

## The five that matter this quarter

1. **11. Become the system of record.** The loudest, most repeated pilot signal
   was a request to fold UpSpeech into the clinic's existing platform. "I'd love
   it to be genuinely part of the therapy, not an extra."
2. **19. The buyer, the champion and the user have three different pains.** One
   product, three messages. The clinic hears capacity, the SLP hears their
   evening back, the patient hears that the week counted.
3. **45. New category, familiar workflow.** Between-session practice with a
   feedback loop is new. The clinic's week is not, and nobody asked us to change
   it.
4. **44. Your call recordings are your best copywriters.** The pilot interviews
   already wrote the positioning. "Practising to a mirror is useless; seeing and
   hearing yourself is the advantage."
5. **23 (rewritten). One ICP.** Clinics onboarding patients who are new to
   therapy. Group 1 did roughly 2.5x the practice and 5x the recordings of
   patients with prior therapy.

## Adopt

**1. Never let your marketing outshine the customer's story.**
The patient's week is the story and the SLP's judgment is the story. The product
is the thing in the background that made both visible. Consent rule 5 in
`brand-voice.md` still gates every real person.

**2. Never build your pipeline on one channel.**
Today the pipeline is one clinic and the founder's network. The three declared
tracks (LinkedIn, Instagram, AEO) are the diversification, and SLP congresses
and university clinics are a fourth that costs nothing but time.

**4. Cut the landing page copy in half. Then again.**
Applies unchanged. A clinician skims.

**5. Reviews and churn stories travel faster than your ads.**
Stronger here than in normal SaaS. The Portuguese SLP world is small enough that
one clinic's opinion reaches the rest of the field, and the stuttering community
is quick to spot a brand using people who stutter as a prop.

**8. Inbound over outbound. Create gravity, don't chase.**
This is what the AEO track is for, alongside LinkedIn. Cold outbound to
clinicians about a clinical tool converts badly and costs credibility.

**9. A free trial beats a feature comparison page.**
The clinic pilot is the free trial, and eight weeks with real patients did more
than any deck would have. Worth making the pilot a repeatable package
(onboarding, assessment cadence, a written read-out) instead of a bespoke effort
each time.

**11. Become the system of record. Integrations are lock-in.**
The pilot's strongest ask. One caveat: the record we own is the practice and
feedback history, and clinical notes may well stay in the clinic's own system.
That argues for integrating with the clinic platform rather than trying to
replace it.

**14. Sales calls are market research. Mine everything.**
Already proven. The pilot interviews produced the positioning (visibility, not
outcomes), the ICP (new patients), and the top roadmap item (the closed feedback
loop). Bias caveat from the investor update: founder-run interviews skew
positive, so keep pairing them with anonymous surveys.

**15. Don't just win the deal. Own the "X vs You" keyword.**
Already an action in `aeo.md`. Comparison and category pages are the cheapest
durable discovery we have.

**16. Don't post daily if you have nothing to say.**
Matches the declared cadence of 2 to 3 LinkedIn posts a week. Volume without
substance costs credibility with clinicians specifically.

**19. The buyer, the champion, and the user have three different pains.**
See the table above. Most of the marketing mistakes available to us come from
sending the patient message to the clinic or the clinic message to the patient.

**20. Positioning is a rental until product-market fit.**
Already exercised once. The July 2026 correction moved us off "stuttering app"
and onto the SLP and patient practice and feedback loop, with stuttering as the
first module. Expect to do it again.

**22. Lose gracefully. The champion buys you at their next company.**
SLPs move between clinics and the field is small. A clinic that says no this
year is a therapist who remembers us at a different clinic next year.

**25. Rebrand before the market decides what you are.**
The July 2026 identity sweep was exactly this, done before "the stuttering app"
set. Same discipline applies to the next label that starts sticking, most likely
"AI report tool".

**32. Sell the after state. Nobody wants a CRM.**
Already in the voice. "Arrive at your session with a week of progress" is the
after state. Nobody wants a practice-logging platform.

**37. Conferences, live drops, industry reports. Theater works.**
In this field the theater is real and cheap: SLP congresses, university clinics,
ISAD on October 22. The pilot read-out is our industry report and it exists
already.

**38. Contrarian strategy, conventional packaging.**
The stutter-positive stance is genuinely contrarian in a market that still sells
fluency. The packaging around it should stay conventionally clinical, which is
what makes the stance land instead of reading as activism with a login.

**41. Don't be "cheaper Salesforce." Be a different game.**
We are not a cheaper documentation tool and not a cheaper teletherapy platform.
The different game is what happens between the sessions.

**42. Convert one influential voice and the flock follows.**
Fluency specialists and SLP educators. Partly true already: one pilot therapist
extended the product to a patient outside the pilot on her own initiative.

**44. Your call recordings are your best copywriters.**
Every quote worth using is already sitting in the pilot interviews. Anonymise,
get written consent, and never use a patient's own recording as a sales prop.

**45. New category, familiar workflow.**
Validated by the pilot ask. The therapists did not want a new place to go, they
wanted the practice data inside the week they already run.

**47. Won the deal? Stop selling.**
Once a clinic signs, the job is onboarding: which patients, which techniques,
what cadence, notifications on. The pilot showed engagement was gated by missing
mechanics rather than weak desire, so the rollout is where the value is won.

**48. Your GTM motion is not your identity. Stay formless.**
Clinic-as-buyer is the current thesis, not a commitment. The patient willingness
to pay signal and the self-management wedge are both live: "my appointments
won't last forever; this is a cycle, and I could use this to practise again in
worse periods without needing new appointments."

## Rewrite

**3. Don't announce the roadmap. Ship, then talk.**
→ _No roadmap in public marketing. Full roadmap to the clinics building it with
us._ The pilot clinic produced the feedback-loop finding precisely because they
knew what was coming and could say what was missing. Treating a pilot partner
like an audience wastes the pilot.

**7. Build a community that creates your content for you.**
→ _Join the community that already exists. Do not farm it._ The stuttering and
neurodiversity communities predate us and are not a content supply. The
`aeo.md` community caveat applies: show up as a participant, never post a
community member's words as marketing without consent.

**10. Fire bad-fit customers before they churn loudly.**
→ _Qualify at the front, and wind down with care._ A clinic cannot be dropped
mid-therapy; its patients are inside a course of care. The filter belongs at
the start: does this clinic have new patients to onboard from day one? If an
exit ever happens it has to include a data export and a handover that leaves no
patient stranded.

**12. Publish your pricing. Admit what you're bad at.**
→ _The second half now, the first half when there is pricing._ There is no
pricing to publish yet, only willingness-to-pay evidence. The admission half is
worth more than usual here: SLPs are earned by honest limits, and the
regulatory posture obliges us to be explicit about what the product does not
claim.

**13. Nobody buys your mission. They buy to look good in their QBR.**
→ _Mission earns the therapist. Numbers earn the clinic._ Speech clinics have no
QBR. The clinic owner wants capacity and a differentiated offer; the SLP wants
to walk in already knowing how the week went. The stutter-positive stance does
real work with clinicians as a values signal, it just does not close the sale.

**17. Category leaders sell the cost of not buying.**
→ _Cost framing is allowed for the clinician's own workload. Never for a
patient's outcome._ Unreviewed submissions piling up and evenings lost to notes
are fair game. "Your child is falling behind" is banned outright by sensitivity
rule 6, and the same applies to any version aimed at an adult patient.

**18. Walled gardens die. Open APIs, public docs, free tiers.**
→ _Integration-first and public documentation. No open free tier holding patient
recordings._ Open integration is the validated demand. A self-serve free tier is
a different matter when the data is patient health data: it implies a data
processing agreement, a retention policy, and a support obligation to a clinic
paying nothing.

**23. One ICP, one channel, one message, until it breaks.**
→ _One ICP. Two audiences, because the product has two._ The B2B2C shape forces
a patient channel and a clinician channel, which is why Instagram and LinkedIn
both exist. The ICP is single and the pilot named it: clinics onboarding
patients who are new to therapy.

**26. Let analysts and customers say what you can't.**
→ _Third-party voices carry credibility, not claims._ Tempting given the claims
constraint, and it needs a hard limit. A testimonial we solicit, edit, or
publish can be treated as our own claim under medical device rules, so a
customer cannot be used to say something about fluency outcomes that we are not
allowed to say ourselves.

**27. Category creation is religion-building. Name the movement.**
→ _Name the practice category. The movement is already named and it is not
ours._ Stutter-positive and neurodiversity are the community's terms, adopted
not invented. What is open to us is naming the thing between sessions.

**29. The campaign isn't done at MQL. Map it to revenue.**
→ _Map to pilots started._ There is no MQL and no revenue line to map to yet.
The terminal metric that exists is a clinic agreeing to a pilot, and every
campaign should be answerable to that rather than to follower counts.

**33. Every persona has one metric they're fired over. Find it.**
→ _Every persona has one thing that ruins their week. Find that._ Nobody is
getting fired over a metric in a five-therapist clinic. The SLP's version is the
evening spent writing notes. The clinic owner's is unused capacity. The
patient's is practising all week and having nothing come back.

**35. Launch into budget season, not into August.**
→ _Launch into the clinical calendar._ September intake at clinics and
university clinics, and ISAD on October 22, are the real windows. August in
Iberia is genuinely dead.

**36. Can't win enterprise? Make "no enterprise" your positioning.**
→ _Name the beachhead instead of the anti-position._ Enterprise here means
hospital groups and national health services, and that is a fight for later, not
one to define ourselves against now. The independent clinic is the beachhead and
saying so plainly is stronger than a posture.

**39. Pick a public fight with the status quo.**
→ _Pick the fight precisely._ The target is the unverifiable "how was your
week?" and the evening lost to session notes. Never the profession, never
another clinician's therapy choices, and never the community's positions on
fluency.

**46. Publish your incidents. Flaws read as truth.**
→ _A status page and honest incident notes to clinics. Not public postmortems on
patient data._ Transparency about downtime is good practice. An incident
touching personal data goes down the GDPR path first, to the controller, which
is the clinic, and to the regulator where required. That route is not a blog
post.

## Reject

**6. Distribution beats product quality in year one.**
The field is small and referral-driven. A clinic that adopts early and hits a
broken flow tells the other clinics, and there is no second attempt with that
network. This law assumes a market big enough to absorb a bad first impression,
which is not the market we are in.

**21. "We're just a simple tool" slips past procurement.**
We hold patient health data. The clinic's data review is the gate we want to
pass properly, because a clinic that discovers the real scope after signing is a
clinic we lose along with its opinion of us. There is a legitimate cousin to
this law: keep the data actually processed minimal, so the review is genuinely
short.

**28. If the launch doesn't scare legal, it's not a launch.**
Directly against the posture. Every value claim rests on visibility, structured
practice, and self-review, none of which assert a change in fluency, and that
restraint is what keeps the product on clean Class I footing with a low
compliance burden. A launch that scares our clinical lead is a launch that gets
pulled.

**30. Never show buyers the implementation pain.**
Clinical rollout has real pain: enrolling patients, agreeing an assessment
cadence, training therapists, turning notifications on. Hiding it produces a
stalled rollout and a champion who feels sold to. Naming it up front is also
what the honest voice requires.

**40. Free users who'd never pay are a cost center, not a funnel.**
Patients are not the funnel. The clinic is the buyer, and patients who never pay
anything are the evidence base, the retention story, and the reason the
therapist champions the product internally. Applying this law would mean
optimising away the users the whole thesis rests on.

## Not yet

Sound advice with nothing to attach it to today. Revisit when the gap closes.

| #   | Law                                                     | What has to exist first                                               |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 24  | Marketing that flatters sales gets budget               | A sales function separate from the founder                            |
| 31  | Three pricing tiers. Design the middle one to win       | Pricing. The WTP evidence is in the pilot analysis when we get there  |
| 34  | Price like the premium option and buyers assume you are | Pricing, plus a decision on whether patients are ever billed directly |
| 43  | Brand makes performance marketing cheap. Cut it last    | Any paid spend at all                                                 |
