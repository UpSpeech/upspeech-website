# UpSpeech Blog Strategy

The blog is the only channel where the work keeps paying after the week it was
published. Instagram and LinkedIn decay in 48 hours. A technique explainer that
ranks, or that an answer engine quotes, keeps bringing people in for years.

Writing rules live in [`../CONTENT_DIRECTIVES.md`](../CONTENT_DIRECTIVES.md).
This file covers what to write and why.

## The job

Two jobs, and they pull in different directions, so keep them in separate posts.

**Be findable.** `aeo.md` records the baseline: zero AI mentions across ChatGPT,
Gemini, Perplexity, Copilot, and Google AI Overviews, and no share of voice on
any non-branded buyer prompt. UpSpeech ranks first for its own name and is
invisible for everything else. The blog is the main lever we control on that.

**Be worth trusting.** A clinician deciding whether to put patients on a tool
reads what the company writes about the field. Writing carefully about stuttering,
about adherence, and about the limits of what the product proves is a credibility
argument that no landing page can make.

## Two preconditions

Both are flagged in `aeo.md` and both cost more than any single article.

1. **Fix the prerender failure on the technique pages.** Eleven technique articles
   plus the index already exist with Article and FAQPage schema, and crawlers see
   an error state instead of the content. That is our richest citable content,
   currently invisible. Publishing new posts that link into broken pages wastes
   the internal linking.
2. **Ship `/for-slps` and `/for-people-who-stutter`.** The blog sends readers
   somewhere. Right now the B2B buyer prompt has no landing page to arrive at.

## Pillars

**1. Technique explainers.** Easy onset, light contact, pull-out, cancellation,
preparatory set, pausing, prolonged speech, voluntary stuttering, identification
and desensitization. Eleven already exist under `/techniques`. Blog posts here go
deeper than the page does: when a clinician chooses this technique, what practice
actually looks like at home, what it feels like to the person doing it. These are
the most quotable pages we will ever have, because they answer a question a person
types.

**2. Between-session practice and adherence.** The differentiated pillar. Nobody
else is writing seriously about why home practice does not happen and what
changes it. Clinician-facing, evidence-aware, honest about what we have measured
and what we have not.

**3. Stutter-positive education.** For people who stutter, parents, and the wider
community. What stutter-positive therapy actually means. What to say and not say
to someone who stutters. When to ask an SLP, written without fear. This pillar
also feeds Instagram, and it is the one most likely to be shared by people who
have nothing to do with buying software.

**4. Clinic operations and documentation.** Buyer-facing. The evening lost to
session notes, what documentation actually has to contain, how clinics think about
capacity. Useful to a clinic owner whether or not they ever buy from us.

**5. Field notes and evidence reviews.** Honest reads of the literature, congress
write-ups, what we learned building with SLPs. Low volume, high credibility.
This is where we can say "the evidence here is thinner than people assume" and be
believed later when we say something positive.

## The first ten

Ordered by impact. The first four map directly to the buyer prompts in `aeo.md`.

1. How to keep practicing speech therapy between sessions
2. What does stutter-positive therapy actually mean?
3. Does AI note-taking work for speech-language pathologists?
4. Best apps for people who stutter in 2026 (honest, competitors included fairly)
5. Easy onset: what it is and how to practice it at home
6. Why home practice does not happen, and what changes it
7. What to say, and not say, to someone who stutters
8. When should a parent ask a speech-language pathologist about stuttering?
9. What goes into a speech therapy session report
10. Light contact and pull-out: two techniques for different moments

Number 4 needs care. An honest roundup that treats competitors fairly is what
answer engines reward and what readers trust. A roundup that puts us first is
worth nothing and reads as an advertisement.

## Cadence

Two posts a month, sustained. Not eight in the first month and none after.

The trap with a blog is starting at a pace that assumes free time that will not
exist once clinic conversations pick up. Two a month, on a schedule, beats a burst
followed by a graveyard, because a blog with nothing published in six months is a
negative trust signal to exactly the clinician we are trying to convince.

Roughly: one findability post (pillar 1 or 3) and one credibility post
(pillar 2, 4, or 5) each month.

## Language

English first, because the site is English and answer engines index the English
prompts we are losing.

Portuguese second, and only for the posts a Portuguese clinician or patient would
plausibly search in Portuguese: the technique explainers, the parent-facing piece,
and the stutter-positive explainer. Those are written again in PT-PT rather than
translated, following the Portuguese section of `CONTENT_DIRECTIVES.md`. A
Portuguese version lives at its own URL with `hreflang` pointing both ways.

Do not translate the clinic-operations or field-notes posts. The Portuguese
clinical audience is small enough to reach directly, and the maintenance cost of a
mirrored blog is higher than it looks.

## How the blog feeds the other channels

One article becomes a LinkedIn document carousel, three Instagram slides, and a
technique story frame. Write the article first, because the long form is where the
thinking happens and the social posts are extractions from it. Going the other way
produces articles that are three captions stapled together.

Never post the article's text as a LinkedIn post and link out. LinkedIn suppresses
external links, and the post reads like an RSS feed. Write a real post about one
idea in the article, and put the link in the first comment or in a follow-up.

## What good looks like

- A technique page cited by an answer engine on a non-branded prompt.
- An SLP sharing an article with a colleague, unprompted.
- The article ranking for the exact question it was written to answer.
- The AEO re-run, quarterly, moving off zero.

Traffic is not the metric. Being quoted is.
