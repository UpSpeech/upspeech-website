# Audiences

Who we write for. Pick exactly one per post. A post aimed at a clinician and a
patient at once lands with neither.

---

## The strategic frame

The people UpSpeech is for, at full width, are:

- Anyone living with a communication, speech, or language difficulty. Children,
  teenagers, and adults.
- The parents and caregivers around them.
- The professionals who work with them.
- The clinics those professionals work in.

**Stuttering is the entry point, not the ceiling.** It is where we start because
it is underserved, because the founder lives it, and because the gap between the
session and the rest of the week is wider in fluency work than almost anywhere
else in the field. Language disorders are the next focus. See
[`proof-library.md`](proof-library.md) for what we are allowed to say about each.

This matters for content in two concrete ways.

**Do not write copy that hard-codes stuttering into the brand.** "The app for
people who stutter" is true today and becomes a rewrite later. "Support that
continues after the session" is true today and stays true. Prefer the second shape
for anything durable: bio lines, boilerplate, the manifesto post, the homepage.
Post-level copy can and should be specific about stuttering, because vague content
persuades nobody.

**Do not claim the wider ground yet either.** We do not serve DLD or speech sound
disorders today, and saying we do is the same failure as any other overclaim.
Content about those conditions is education about the field, clearly framed as
such.

## Persona 1: the person with a communication difficulty

Today this means a person who stutters. The persona is written so the next
condition slots in without a rewrite.

**Who.** A child, a teenager, or an adult. In therapy now, between courses of
therapy, or on a waiting list. Often has been through this before.

**The job they hire content for.** To feel less alone with it, and to find
something practical they can actually do this week.

**What they already believe.** That they have tried things. That progress is slow
and not linear. That most content about speech is written by people who do not
have this, and is either pitying or selling a cure.

**Their real objection.** "Another app." Followed closely by "this will tell me to
speak more smoothly and I am tired of being told that."

**Their vocabulary vs ours.**

| They often say | We write |
| --- | --- |
| my stutter, my speech | your speech, the technique you are working on |
| a bad speech day | a harder day |
| blocking, getting stuck | blocking, a block (their word, and it is the right one) |
| fluent | steadier, easier (we do not treat fluent as the goal) |
| I sound stupid | not repeated back, ever. Answer the feeling, not the label. |

Identity-first and person-first are both accepted by the community. "People who
stutter" and "person who stutters" are ours. "Stutterer" as a label is not, and
"suffers from" is not.

**Where they are.** Instagram first, and in community spaces we are guests in
rather than owners of.

**What makes them leave.** Any framing of fluency as the goal. Pity. Inspiration
posing. A stat about them used to sell something. Somebody imitating a stutter,
however affectionately meant.

**The sentence that lands.** "Speaking up matters more than speaking smoothly."

**What changes by age.**

- **Children** are reached through a parent. Write to the parent, keep the child
  in the room, and never let a post about a child be read over their shoulder as
  a description of a problem.
- **Teenagers** are reached through autonomy. What they want is control over their
  own practice and no adult narrating their progress. Copy that sounds like a
  school report loses them instantly.
- **Adults** are reached through a specific situation they are dreading. A phone
  call, an introduction, an interview, ordering out loud. Name the situation and
  they will recognize themselves.

## Persona 2: the parent or caregiver

**Who.** Usually a parent of a child aged 3 to 10, often in the first year of
noticing. Sometimes an adult child of an older parent after a stroke.

**The job they hire content for.** To find out whether this is serious, whether
they caused it, and what to do next.

**What they already believe.** That they may be overreacting. Or that they left it
too late. Frequently both in the same week.

**Their real objection.** "Is this something to worry about or not?" Everything
else is downstream of that question.

**Their vocabulary vs ours.**

| They say | We write |
| --- | --- |
| will he grow out of it | most children who start stuttering stop, and there is no way to tell which from the outside, which is why asking early helps |
| is it my fault | it is not caused by parenting, and it is not caused by anything you did |
| speech therapist | terapeuta da fala in PT-PT, speech and language therapist in EN |
| fix, cure, get rid of | work on, practice, support |
| falling behind | never used |

**What makes them leave.** Fear hooks. "Is your child falling behind?" is banned
outright by the sensitivity rules and it is also the single most common thing
this category of marketing does. We never do it, and being the brand that does not
is itself the differentiator with this reader.

**The sentence that lands.** "Asking early is not the same as something being
wrong."

## Persona 3: the speech and language therapist

**Who.** Practicing, in a clinic, a school, a hospital, or private practice.
Carrying a caseload and a documentation load. Frequently the person who decides
whether a tool enters the therapeutic relationship at all.

**The job they hire content for.** To find out whether we understand their job,
and whether this will make their week better or add to it.

**What they already believe.** That home practice mostly does not happen. That
notes eat their evenings. That most technology sold to them was designed by people
who have never run a session, and promises outcomes it cannot support.

**Their real objection.** Two, in order: "does this replace my judgment?" and
"where is the evidence?" The honest answers are no and it is early, and both
answers earn more trust than a confident overclaim.

**Their vocabulary vs ours.** They use the clinical terms precisely and they
notice when we do not. Caseload, adherence, carryover, generalization, session
notes, discharge. Getting one wrong costs more credibility than a whole post
earns. When unsure, describe the thing rather than reaching for the term.

**Where they are.** LinkedIn, professional associations, congresses, and each
other.

**What makes them leave.** An outcome claim. "AI that assesses speech."
Patronizing simplification of their own field. A patient used as a sales asset.
Anything that treats documentation as a personality flaw rather than a system
problem.

**The sentence that lands.** "The AI drafts. You decide."

## Persona 4: the clinic

**Who.** Owner, clinical coordinator, or practice manager. Sometimes the same
person as persona 3, wearing a second hat and thinking differently.

**The job they hire content for.** To work out whether this changes capacity,
continuity, or admin load enough to be worth the disruption of introducing it.

**What they already believe.** That software rollouts fail on adoption, not on
features. That their therapists are the constraint and are already at capacity.
That patient data is a liability they are personally responsible for.

**Their real objection.** "What does this cost me in time before it saves me
any?" And, unspoken: "what happens to the data?"

**What makes them leave.** Vagueness about data handling. Enterprise language
aimed at a four-person clinic. A price quoted before anyone understands how they
work, which is one of the reasons we do not quote prices at all.

**The sentence that lands.** "The week between sessions stops being invisible."

## Persona 5: the wider community

**Who.** People connected to stuttering and neurodiversity who are not customers
and never will be. Advocates, educators, family members, and people who simply
care about how this gets talked about.

**The job they hire content for.** To find something worth sharing that gets it
right.

**What they already believe.** That brands in this space get it wrong by default,
and that the correction is worth making publicly.

**Their real objection.** "Are you selling something with this?" And the answer
has to be no, actually, for the posts aimed at them.

**What makes them leave.** A product mention inside an education post. The
education pillar carries nothing to sell, and that is a strategy decision, not
modesty: a soft product mention in a myth-busting post costs more reach than the
mention is worth.

**The sentence that lands.** Whatever is true, said plainly, with nothing attached
to it.

## Using this file

The four-line brief in `../CONTENT_DIRECTIVES.md` starts with **Reader**. That
line takes exactly one persona name from this file. Not two. Not "SLPs and
patients". If a post genuinely serves two, it is two posts, written twice, and
`repurposing.md` covers how.
